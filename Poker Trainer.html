🎯 The Goal

Texas Hold'em is a betting game played with a standard 52-card deck. You get 2 private cards (your hole cards) and share 5 community cards dealt face-up in the middle (the board).

Your final hand is the best 5-card poker hand you can make from any combination of your 2 cards and the 5 community cards — you can use both of your cards, just one, or even none ("playing the board").

You win a hand — and the pot of chips — in one of two ways:

  • Showdown: after all the betting, you have the best 5-card hand of the players remaining.
  • Everyone folds: you bet, nobody calls, you take the pot — no matter what cards you held. This is why bluffing is possible.
Big idea: Poker isn't about winning the most hands — it's about winning big pots when you're strong and losing small ones when you're weak. Folding is a winning move most of the time.

🔄 How a Hand Plays Out

  1. The button & blinds. A dealer button (Ⓓ) marks the "dealer" and moves one seat left every hand. The player left of the button posts the small blind and the next player the big blind — forced bets that seed the pot so there's something to fight for.
  2. Hole cards. Everyone gets 2 cards face down.
  3. Pre-flop betting. Starting left of the big blind, each player may fold, call (match the current bet), or raise. The big blind gets the last option and may check if nobody raised.
  4. The Flop. 3 community cards are dealt face up. A betting round follows, starting with the first player left of the button still in the hand. When there's no bet yet, you may check (pass) or bet.
  5. The Turn. A 4th community card, then another betting round.
  6. The River. The 5th and final community card, then the last betting round.
  7. Showdown. Remaining players reveal their cards; best 5-card hand takes the pot. If two hands are identical, the pot splits.

The actions

  • Check — pass the action without betting (only when nobody has bet this round).
  • Bet — put chips in (minimum bet = one big blind).
  • Call — match the current bet to stay in.
  • Raise — increase the bet. A raise must be at least as big as the previous bet or raise.
  • Fold — throw your hand away and give up any chips you've already put in.
  • All-in — bet all your remaining chips. You can only win from each opponent up to what you put in (extra bets form side pots).

🧭 Position: The Hidden Superpower

The later you act in a betting round, the more information you have — you've seen what everyone else did before deciding. The button is the best seat: it acts last on every round after the flop.

  • Early position (first to act): play only strong hands — you'll act blind while everyone else reacts to you.
  • Late position (button and just before it): you can profitably play more hands, steal blinds, and control pot size.
  • The blinds: you already have chips in, but you'll be first to act every round after the flop — the worst spot. Don't defend them with junk.
Rule of thumb: the same hand that's a fold in early position can be a raise on the button. When in doubt out of position, tighten up.

🃏 Which Starting Hands Should I Play?

There are 169 distinct starting hands, and most are losers. The #1 beginner leak is playing too many hands. Here's a simple tier list:

Premium — AA, KK, QQ, AK. Always raise; happily re-raise.
Strong — JJ, TT, 99, AQ, AJ suited, KQ suited. Usually raise; be more careful if someone re-raises.
Playable — mid pairs (88–66), suited aces (A9s–A2s), suited connectors like J-10s, 10-9s. Best in late position, cheaply. These make big disguised hands (or easy folds).
Fold the rest — including tempting junk like K-9 offsuit, Q-7, J-5, and the infamous 7-2. Unsuited, disconnected cards make weak pairs that lose big pots.

Beginner pre-flop plan

  • If you're first in, come in for a raise (about 3 big blinds) — don't just call ("limp").
  • Facing a raise? Fold everything but Strong/Premium hands. Re-raise with Premium.
  • Expect to fold roughly 3 out of every 4 hands before the flop. That's not passive — that's how winners play.

🧮 The Math You Actually Need

Outs

An out is a card that improves you to (probably) the best hand. Example: you hold and the flop is . Any heart gives you the nut flush — 13 hearts minus the 4 you can see = 9 outs.

The Rule of 4 and 2

  • × 2 per card: each single card to come hits your draw about outs × 2% of the time. (9 outs ≈ 19% on the turn card; a 4-out gutshot ≈ 8%)
  • × 4 for both cards: if you'll see the turn and river for this one price — you're all-in on the flop, or nobody will bet again — it's ≈ outs × 4%. (9 outs ≈ 36%)

When someone bets the flop, your call usually only buys the next card (they can bet again on the turn), so compare the price to the × 2 number unless the call is for all the chips.

Pot odds

When facing a bet, compare the price to your chances. If the pot is $80 and you must call $20, you're paying 20 into a final pot of 100 — you need to win 20% of the time to break even.

A flush draw (~19% for the next card) is right at that price — and in practice it's a fine call, because when the flush hits you usually win more bets on later streets (implied odds). A gutshot (~8% per card) is nowhere near the price — fold it to big bets.

Shortcut: needed % = call ÷ (pot after your call). The coach in the Play tab computes this for you every time you face a bet — watch how it compares to your equity.

💡 Ten Beginner Tips

  1. Fold more. Tight is right while you learn. Most hands are unplayable.
  2. Raise, don't limp. If a hand is worth playing, it's usually worth raising.
  3. Bet your strong hands. Beginners trap too much. Strong hands want big pots — build one.
  4. Respect big cards on the board. If the board shows A-K-Q and you have a pair of 9s, you're probably beat.
  5. Kickers matter. A-7 vs A-K on an ace-high board is a disaster for A-7. This is why weak aces are folds.
  6. Count your outs and use the rule of 4 and 2 before calling with a draw.
  7. Position, position, position. Play more hands late, fewer early.
  8. Watch the other players, not just your cards. Who bets big only with the goods? Who bluffs?
  9. Don't tilt. Bad beats happen — good decisions lose sometimes. Judge yourself on decisions, not results.
  10. Never play with money you can't afford to lose. This app is free — practice here first!

📚 Glossary

All-inBetting every chip you have.
BlindsForced bets posted before cards are dealt (small blind and big blind).
BoardThe face-up community cards everyone shares.
ButtonThe marker for the (nominal) dealer — the best position at the table.
Check-raiseChecking, then raising after an opponent bets — a power move.
DrawAn unmade hand that needs another card, e.g. four to a flush.
Flop / Turn / RiverThe 3rd–5th community cards: flop (3 at once), turn (4th), river (5th).
GutshotAn inside straight draw — only 4 cards complete it (e.g. 9-8 on a 6-5-2 board needs a 7). A double gutshot has two different inside cards that complete it (8 outs).
Heads-upA pot (or game) with only two players.
KickerA side card that breaks ties, e.g. the K in A-K vs A-7 on an ace-high board.
LimpJust calling the big blind pre-flop instead of raising. Usually weak.
MuckFolding / discarding your hand without showing it.
NutsThe best possible hand given the board. "The nut flush" = best possible flush.
Offsuit / SuitedWhether your two hole cards share a suit. Suited hands can make flushes.
Open-endedA straight draw completed on either end — 8 outs (e.g. 9-8 on 7-6-2).
OutsCards left in the deck that improve your hand to a likely winner.
Pocket pairA pair in your hole cards, e.g. two 8s ("pocket eights").
Pot oddsThe price of a call vs. the size of the pot — the math of whether calling is profitable.
Set / TripsThree of a kind. A "set" uses a pocket pair + one board card (very disguised, very strong).
ShowdownRevealing hands after the final betting round to decide the winner.
Side potA separate pot created when someone is all-in and others keep betting.
TiltPlaying badly because you're emotional after a loss.
